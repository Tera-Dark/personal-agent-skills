# Painterly Game Illustration Grammar — 若干爪作品的可迁移设计机制

> Purpose: calibrate composition, environmental light, color organization and information hierarchy for premium anime-game illustrations. This is a study of transferable design decisions, not a request to reproduce any single artwork or copy its character-specific surface details. Adapt the mechanisms to the current character and brief.

## Core thesis

The image feels expensive when **figure, movement, environment, light and ornament behave as one designed system**. Richness comes from relationships—not from adding more props, particles, adjectives or background objects.

This mode is useful for premium character cards, illustrated key visuals, dynamic character portraits and painterly narrative scenes. It is not the default for every white-background standee or restrained editorial portrait.

## 1. Build a readable three-scale composition

### Macro: establish the image before the detail
- Select 3–4 dominant masses: the protagonist, one counter-mass or secondary figure echo, one environment / graphic mass, and one quiet field.
- Prefer a committed diagonal, circular sweep, rising arc or opposing two-axis flow. The pose, hair, sleeves, ribbons, scenery and framing shapes should share or deliberately counter this vector.
- Use asymmetric framing, purposeful crop, foreshortening and foreground overlap to create depth. Every overlap must clarify placement or movement.
- The silhouette should remain attractive and readable when reduced to a thumbnail. Micro-detail cannot repair a weak pose or unclear large shape.

### Meso: make character and setting interlock
- Give the body a task: reaching, turning, leaning, looking back, lifting, reclining, balancing or responding to something in the scene.
- Let hair, fabric, straps, props and environmental forms continue the motion rather than act as unrelated ornaments.
- A secondary visual echo may be an enlarged portrait, reflection, sculpture, shadow, circular structure or graphic arc. Use it only when it adds scale, identity or narrative; never add a giant ghost-face by habit.
- Background architecture, landscape, water, amusement structures or ritual objects should change the framing, perspective or color balance—not merely fill empty space.

### Micro: concentrate precision where the eye needs it
- Put the sharpest, most specific detail around the face, hands, signature garment construction and one narrative object.
- Let secondary architecture and foliage resolve into broader color/value shapes, with selective hard accents.
- A dense region should have a clear owner and purpose; nearby regions must simplify enough for the focal point to breathe.

## 2. Treat light as a connector, not an effect

The environment has a real lighting condition: open daylight, sunset, backlit mist, a night city, a stage or a dark interior. Choose the condition first, then let it affect every object.

- Name the source direction and quality: low warm sun, broad skylight, cool window light, reflected water light, or a small practical lamp.
- Use warm/cool interaction intentionally: warm light on lit planes, environment-colored bounce on shadow planes, and a smaller neutral or saturated highlight where material warrants it.
- Let reflected light cross skin, hair, fabric and nearby objects in a physically consistent path. The character should feel lit by the scene, not cut out and pasted on.
- Separate material responses: skin has broad soft transitions; satin has long directional glints; metal has compact hard highlights; glass shows overlap and transmitted color; hair is a coherent mass with selected strand accents.
- Keep a hierarchy of hard, soft and lost edges. Preserve decisive focal edges around eyes, fingertips and key props while allowing selected hair, fabric and background edges to merge into light.
- Atmosphere is allowed when it explains depth or illumination. Global bloom, haze, bokeh and random glitter must not replace light structure.

## 3. Organize color as masses before local hues

- Assign jobs to a small number of large color families: a dominant field, a counter-color, a deep anchor and one restrained accent.
- Bright and pastel palettes still need value anchors. A pale warm sky, cool cyan water and deep ultramarine silhouette can coexist because their areas and values are deliberately unequal.
- Use local environmental color on the character: reflected cyan on shadow-facing skin or fabric, warm rim light along the silhouette, or muted ambient color in the dark folds.
- Make the accent recur with purpose—such as a ribbon, jewel, flower core or graphic line—so the eye follows a route rather than bouncing randomly.
- Do not hard-code teal/orange, blue/pink, gold/black or any signature palette. Recalculate the palette from the character, setting and emotional beat every time.

## 4. Define effective and ineffective information

Effective detail does at least one job:
- tells the character's identity or role;
- clarifies the material or construction;
- establishes depth, scale, movement or a physical relation;
- carries the event or a small story clue;
- reinforces the color rhythm or visual route;
- explains how the light touches a surface.

Ineffective detail does none of these and merely occupies pixels: unrelated charms, repeated ornaments, random floating motifs, texture scattered evenly across the image, or background objects that do not support the composition.

For any dense illustration, decide:
1. what must be read first;
2. what rewards a closer look;
3. what can dissolve into broad shapes.
Do not give all three the same contrast or rendering intensity.

## 5. Make atmosphere emerge from a moment

- Capture the fraction of a second in which the pose, gaze or hand action is changing.
- Let one object or environmental event carry the story. The viewer should be able to infer the moment from gesture, light, material state or spatial relationship without a paragraph of explanation.
- Emotion is stronger when visible through a body decision: a reaching hand, a turned shoulder, a skirt caught by wind, a face looking into light, or an object protected against the body.
- Avoid melodramatic stacking: tears + wind + sunset + particles + dramatic eyes. One precise gesture and its environmental consequence can carry more feeling.

## 6. Camera and illustration design must cooperate

- Use a clear camera decision: eye-level intimacy, slightly low-angle presence, close foreground perspective, oblique overhead view, or a wide environmental frame.
- Portrait scale may be interrupted by a hand, sleeve, prop, railing, leaf, ribbon or architectural edge in the foreground. Keep the face and gesture readable.
- Use repeated curves or arcs to join the face, body and environment; use perspective lines and light bands to lead toward the focal action.
- If the work is intended as a poster, typography or a signature can ride an existing motion path or quiet field. Do not paste text into the sky after the image is designed.
- A cinematic result is not automatically dark or desaturated. Daylight, clean pastels and saturated color can have cinematic depth when perspective, light direction, value hierarchy and crop are deliberate.

## 7. Recommended decision sequence

Before writing a prompt, define in this order:

1. **Story beat:** what is the character doing in this exact half-second?
2. **Camera:** framing, angle, crop and foreground overlap.
3. **Motion path:** the main diagonal / curve shared by pose, hair, fabric and setting.
4. **Mass map:** protagonist, counter-mass, environment shape and quiet field.
5. **Light condition:** source, direction, softness, bounce and cast shadows.
6. **Color jobs:** dominant field, counter-color, deep anchor and single accent.
7. **Information map:** focal detail, secondary detail and broad simplified zones.
8. **Material behavior:** how the key fabric, hair, metal, glass, water or stone responds to light.
9. **Story punctum:** one small concrete detail that rewards a second look.
10. **Compression:** remove any noun that does not support identity, composition, light, action or narrative.

The resulting prompt should express those decisions with concrete placements and relationships. Do not rely on “masterpiece”, “cinematic”, “intricate”, “beautiful lighting” or “highly detailed” to do the design work.

## 8. Failure modes to reject

- Attractive character over a separately rendered background with no shared motion or light.
- A close-up portrait pasted behind a small full figure without a compositional or narrative reason.
- Every strand, ornament, leaf and building receiving equally sharp detail.
- Lots of color, but no clear dominant mass, dark anchor or focal accent.
- Warm/yellow wash spreading everywhere; soft focus erasing the edges that should carry the design.
- A dark poster formula applied to a scene that needs lively, luminous color.
- Floating flowers, feathers, sparks, bokeh and ribbons with no physical, narrative or directional relationship.
- Repeated “dynamic / cinematic / atmosphere” adjectives standing in for camera angle, pose mechanics, light source and actual object placement.
