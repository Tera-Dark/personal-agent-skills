## Core thesis

**A painterly scene feels alive when one character gesture sets the environment in motion, and the resulting motion also organizes color, light and detail.**

Do not treat this as “dark blue + red ribbons + maple leaves + cinematic lighting.” Those are surface features of one image. The transferable mechanism is an authored circulation of visual energy.

## 1. One motion system, several material expressions

In the inspected 《沉契凝锁》 piece, the umbrella canopy forms a large circular mass behind the figure; the long red fabric sweeps across the body and outward; water ripples echo the curve around the feet; scattered leaves repeat the accent color along that path. These parts do not merely coexist: they complete and reinforce a shared rotational movement.

Transfer rules:
- Decide the main motion geometry first: orbit, spiral, diagonal sweep, falling arc, rising current or opposing curves.
- Make the body pose create that movement. Let hair, sleeve, sash, weapon, umbrella, smoke, water, foliage or architecture continue it.
- Repeat the movement at different scales: one large contour, two or three medium supporting curves, then a few small echoes.
- Allow one or two shapes to oppose the motion, so the image has tension rather than becoming a perfectly regular pinwheel.
- Every decorative shape must either continue, counter, frame or punctuate the flow. Remove shapes that do none of these jobs.

Reject:
- hair, ribbons, leaves and effects each flowing in unrelated directions;
- circular swirls added as generic “dynamic energy”;
- a character pasted into a background whose motion does not respond to her pose.

## 2. The character can be smaller than the motion—but must remain the anchor

The inspected work does not rely on enlarging the figure until she fills the canvas. The umbrella, sweeping red fabric, water circle and dark environmental masses form a larger graphic structure around her. Her placement and silhouette remain readable because the high-contrast accents and surrounding light point back toward the figure.

Transfer rules:
- Establish three readable levels: the character silhouette; a large framing / motion shape; and the broad environmental field.
- Use size contrast with a reason. A prop or environmental shape may be larger than the figure if it defines the event, movement or symbolic space.
- Preserve a clear body silhouette against adjacent values. Don't let every strand and fold merge into the background.
- Use the brightest or sharpest local detail to return the eye to the face, hands or action.
- Avoid reducing the character to an unrecognizable speck in a beautiful environment; scale contrast must enhance, not erase, her presence.

## 3. Value structure: dark perimeter, illuminated action

In the direct sample, the deep blue-violet surroundings act as a value anchor. A pale water / reflected-light region opens around the central figure and feet, while warmer lantern points sit farther out. The lighting establishes both depth and a route for reading the image.

Transfer rules:
- Plan a grayscale hierarchy before color detail: dark outer masses, a middle-value character / environment structure, and one connected light region around the action.
- The brightest area should have a reason: wet ground catching the lamp, a window opening, snow, reflected water, stage light or another physical surface.
- Place small warm light points to establish scale and depth; do not sprinkle glowing dots evenly.
- Keep the face, gesture and key prop readable within the main light hierarchy.
- Decide which contour is hard, soft or lost. The silhouette needs selected crisp edges, while distant water, fog, foliage or cloth can dissolve into broader brushwork.

Avoid using “cinematic lighting” as a substitute for saying where the light is, what it illuminates, what casts the shadow, and where the brightest region falls.

## 4. Color roles, not a fixed palette

The visible sample organizes deep indigo / blue-violet shadow, red fabric and leaves, pale lavender / ivory forms, and small amber lights. Red repeats along the movement path; the warm lamps punctuate rather than compete with the red. The lesson is not that every work should be red-and-blue.

Before choosing hues, assign roles:
- **Dominant field:** the broad color mass that sets the scene's emotional temperature.
- **Structural anchor:** dark or lower-saturation forms that keep the composition legible.
- **Counter-color:** the material or light that contrasts with the dominant field.
- **Punctum:** a small accent repeated at meaningful points along the visual path.
- **Light color:** the actual color of the illumination and environmental bounce, not an overlay placed uniformly over the entire image.

The color groups must differ in area and value. Two saturated colors at equal intensity across the whole canvas produce noise; a small, controlled accent can guide the eye through a much richer image.

Do not hard-code red / blue, teal / orange, night palettes or autumn motifs. Recalculate each color role for the character and scene.

## 5. Environment as an active compositional partner

The water in the sample is more than scenery: its pale circular reflection opens the center, defines the figure's ground, creates depth under the feet and echoes the umbrella / fabric curves. Leaves reinforce the red movement rather than forming a generic border.

For any scene element, ask which job it performs:
- **Frame:** contains or crops the figure.
- **Counter-mass:** balances the figure's silhouette.
- **Movement echo:** repeats or redirects the pose's motion.
- **Light receiver:** catches or reflects the source light.
- **Story evidence:** implies weather, time, an action or a previous event.
- **Scale cue:** tells the viewer how large or close the character is.

An element may perform more than one job. If it performs none, remove it.

## 6. Painterly finish through selective resolution

The sample combines clearly readable character / costume shapes with more broken, textured rendering in the water and dark surroundings. Not every surface receives the same kind of edge or the same amount of micro-detail.

Transfer rules:
- Block the full value and color masses before describing fine materials.
- Keep face, fingers, signature garment construction and key prop legible; let secondary scenery be expressed through grouped brush marks.
- Use different brush / edge behavior for different materials: broken water reflections, broad fabric shadows, sharper metal glints, softer skin transitions, coherent hair masses with selected strands.
- Allow limited sketch texture and edge variation where they contribute to material and motion.
- Put dense detail in selected pockets; don't texture every square inch.
- Preserve form under color: a bright palette does not eliminate contact shadows, and a painterly edge is not an excuse for unclear anatomy.

Reject global blur, uniform detail, random sparkles and detail added to rescue a weak silhouette.

## 7. Atmosphere comes from a physical moment

The character should be caught during an action, not simply posed in a mood. In the inspected piece, the tilted composition, raised umbrella, extending fabric and disturbed water imply motion through the space. The environmental effects support that event.

Prompt decisions should therefore specify:
1. the exact half-second of action;
2. the body gesture and weight shift;
3. the main motion curve shared with hair / cloth / prop;
4. the light source and what it touches;
5. one piece of environmental evidence that makes the moment specific.

Do not pile up rain, petals, fog, glowing dust and dramatic expression. A few integrated effects are stronger than many unrelated effects.

## 8. Practical prompt sequence

Use this order for painterly narrative / dynamic game illustrations:

1. Character identity and recognizable silhouette.
2. Camera angle, crop and figure placement.
3. Main motion geometry.
4. Large counter-mass / framing shape.
5. Environment that physically responds to the action.
6. Value map and light source.
7. Color roles and accent path.
8. Costume / hair material response.
9. Selective edge, brush and texture hierarchy.
10. One narrative punctum.
11. Compression: remove decorative nouns that do not support identity, motion, light, scale or story.

Use concrete relationships: “the red sash arcs from the raised shoulder and completes the umbrella's circular contour” is more useful than “flowing, dynamic, cinematic.” The prompt must name placements and relationships, not only moods.

## 9. Keep this branch separate from 小画师 white-background portraits

This painterly-story mode is suited to dynamic character key visuals and environment-led illustrations. It should not automatically control a clean half-body commission portrait.

- For white / pale-background portraits, the small-artist portrait brain remains primary: face, hair silhouette, garment construction and attached detail clusters.
- For painterly narrative scenes, use this document: motion geometry, environmental response, light / value hierarchy and selective painterly resolution.
- If the user requests both, keep the portrait's face and costume appeal as the anchor while borrowing only the scene's chosen motion or light mechanism.
